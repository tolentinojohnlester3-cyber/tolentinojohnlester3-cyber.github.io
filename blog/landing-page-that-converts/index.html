How to create a landing page that actually converts

A landing page is not a prettier homepage. It is one URL with one job: take the promise that earned the click and turn it into a lead, a booking, or a sale. Rankings without that job are a cost. This is the SXO layer I run after SEO, AEO, and GEO — the page that pays for the traffic.

Key takeaway

A landing page is one URL with one job. Match the search snippet, put proof and one CTA above the fold, keep the form short, and make mobile fast. Rankings are a cost until that page converts.

Traffic is not the win

I still see client decks celebrate sessions. Sessions are the input. The output is the form submit, the WhatsApp tap, the booked call. If a page ranks for “install WordPress cPanel” and the visitor lands on a brand story with a newsletter box, the click was wasted — even if Search Console looks healthy.

That gap is search experience optimization (SXO). In layers of SEO, SXO is the last layer: SEO finds you, AEO and GEO get you cited, SXO turns the visit into a customer. Pretty layouts without a job fail this layer. Ugly pages with a clear offer often win it.

A converting landing page restates the query, proves you can do the job, and offers one next step. Extra nav, extra CTAs, and extra stories are how the click dies.

One page, one job

Write the job in one sentence before you open the builder: “This URL exists so a person who wants X does Y.” Examples from work I actually ship:

  • Want a WordPress install on cPanel → follow the steps / hire the setup.
  • Want a GMB that ranks in the map pack → request the audit.
  • Want a clone of a live site onto a new domain → start the migration brief.

If you cannot name Y, you do not have a landing page. You have a brochure. Brochures leak paid and organic traffic. I keep global nav light or gone on campaign URLs. The logo can go home. Everything else should push the same action.

Above the fold: five jobs on one screen

The first screen has to finish the argument. Scroll is optional. On a phone that means the headline, a line of proof, and the button must be visible without a hunt.

  1. Promise. Headline + subhead. “You came for X. Here is X.” Keep it under two lines.
  2. Proof. Stars, a logo strip, a named metric, or a short quote. Proof belongs before the fold, not in a footer nobody reaches.
  3. Offer. What they get if they act — audit, PDF, booking, demo — in concrete words. “Free” without a noun is not an offer.
  4. Action. One primary CTA. Same label in the hero and after proof. “Get started” is weak. “Send the cPanel brief” is a job.
  5. Friction check. Short form, fast LCP, tap-friendly button, visible privacy and a real person behind the brand.

Hero videos that autoplay, carousels, and dual popups steal the first three seconds. I would rather ship a static hero with a sharp H1 than a motion reel that hides the button on a mid-range Android.

Message match from click to fold

The most expensive conversion bug I still find is a mismatch: the Google snippet or ad promised one job, the H1 sold another. People do not “explore the brand.” They check whether they landed in the right place. If the words do not match, they hit back. That bounce is not a quality problem in the algorithm’s eyes as often as it is a copy problem in yours.

Practical rule: copy the target query into the H1, then add the outcome. If the query is “Google Business Profile optimization,” the H1 is not “We love local.” It is “Google Business Profile optimization for the map pack” plus a subhead that names reviews, categories, and posts. The button continues that sentence.

Paid traffic makes this non-negotiable. Organic is the same physics with a slower invoice. I treat title tag, meta description, H1, and CTA as one string with three lengths — not four separate creative briefs.

Proof that lowers risk

A landing page asks a stranger to give a number, an email, or a calendar slot. They will not do that on a stock photo of a handshake. Put one of these above the fold:

  • A specific result (“indexed 14 service pages in 21 days”) with the client category, not a secret brand if you cannot name it
  • Review score + count from a property you control (Google, Clutch, or the site itself)
  • Logos of properties you actually handled — and can show in the portfolio
  • A named author with a photo and a city. For this site that is me in Sta. Rosa, Laguna. Anonymous agencies convert worse on high-intent searches.

Fake counters and “trusted by 10,000+” with no source train people to bounce. One true number beats a wall of badges.

Forms and friction

Every extra field is a tax. I start with the minimum I need to reply: name, contact, and the URL they care about. Qualification questions can live on the thank-you page or in the first email. Phone-only forms lose people who will type an email. Email-only forms lose people who live in Viber and WhatsApp. Offer the channel they already used to find you when you can.

  • Button contrast: orange on this site’s palette (#ff6b1a) on white, large enough for a thumb
  • No “Submit.” Use the job: “Send the brief,” “Book the 20-min audit”
  • Error states that name the missing field, not a generic red ring
  • A thank-you URL you can fire an analytics event on — not a JavaScript overlay that never loads as a pageview

If legal needs a checkbox, keep it. If marketing wants industry, budget, timeline, and a paragraph before they have given anything, move those fields. You can ask after they have a reason to trust you.

Speed and mobile

A converting layout on a three-second LCP is still a losing page. People who came from Search or ads are impatient by default. I treat landing URLs like money pages in the load-speed guide: compress the hero, lazy-load below-the-fold shots, skip heavy sliders, and keep third-party scripts off the first paint.

Mobile is not a shrunk desktop. Thumb zone for the CTA. Type at 16px+ so iOS does not zoom the form. Click-to-call if the offer is a conversation. If the fold on a 390px screen is a 900px hero image and no button, the design is not done.

SEO still matters on a landing page

Some teams strip landing pages of indexability “because they are for ads.” That is a choice, not a law. If the URL answers a real query, I index it. Unique title, H1, a short extract for AEO, and sensible internal links from the blog. Thin doorway copies of the same offer on twenty URLs is the pattern Google’s spam systems already punish — see the August 2026 spam update.

Do not hide the offer in an image. Crawlers and AI engines extract text, tables, and named facts. A hero that is only a JPEG of your headline will rank and convert worse than HTML type. Schema helps when it matches the page: FAQ if you have real questions, HowTo if it is a procedure, not a fake review star dump.

What I test before I call a page “live”

  1. One job. Read the URL out loud. If you need “and” twice, split the page.
  2. Proof above the fold. A number, a name, or a logo. Not a stock mountain.
  3. One primary CTA. Same label, repeated. Secondary links go below.
  4. Short form. Name + contact unless the value clearly requires more.
  5. Fast on mobile. Check LCP on a real phone, not only PageSpeed’s lab URL.
  6. Test the offer. Change one thing: headline, button copy, or field count. Do not restyle the whole page and call it a test.

Measure the thank-you page, not the button click alone. Clicks without a completed submit are still a leak. Search Console tells you the query. Analytics tells you whether that query paid rent.

If the page already ranks and does not convert, do not start with a new color theme. Start with message match, proof, and the CTA label. Design is last.

Landing page conversion FAQ

Should a landing page have a full website menu?

Not on a campaign or high-intent offer URL. Keep a logo that goes home and the primary action. Extra menu items are exits you paid for.

How long should a landing page be?

As long as the risk. A low-cost lead magnet can convert in one screen. A high-ticket service needs proof, process, and FAQs below the fold — still with one CTA, not a new job every section.

Do I need a separate landing page for every keyword?

No. One strong page per job. Close variants should consolidate. Doorway pages that swap one city name and nothing else are spam risk, not SXO.

What is a good conversion rate?

It depends on traffic source and offer. I compare the URL to itself after a change, and to similar offers on the same site. A 2% form rate on cold organic can be healthy; a 0.3% rate on branded paid search usually means the page lost the message.

Should landing pages be noindexed?

Only if they are true ad duplicates or thin tests. If the page answers a search, index it, give it a unique title, and link it from a related guide so it is not an orphan.

Where does this sit next to GEO and AEO?

AEO and GEO earn the mention. The landing page has to convert the people who still click through. Build the extractable answer, then give that visitor a job to do.
