Parasite SEO explained: earned placements vs spam

Parasite SEO is ranking a page on someone else’s domain because that host already has authority. A journalist writing about you and a LinkedIn article can look similar on the SERP. Paying a newspaper to host a casino or loan page is the version Google now treats as site reputation abuse.

What parasite SEO is

In SEO slang, a parasite page is a URL you publish (or pay to publish) on a domain you do not own, so Google can treat that URL as part of a trusted host. The “parasite” is your commercial page. The host is the newspaper, university, publisher, or platform whose links and brand already move rankings.

The tactic is older than the name. Guest posts, Medium articles, LinkedIn Pulse, YouTube descriptions, and “contributor” slots on magazines all sit on the same spectrum: you are not ranking your domain. You are ranking a path on theirs. For the basics of how Google crawls and ranks any URL, see what SEO is.

Two things are true at once:

  • Off-site pages can be the honest way a brand shows up — press, profiles, videos, expert quotes.
  • Buying a commercial section on an unrelated host, just to steal that host’s authority, is the pattern Google named site reputation abuse.
I do not sell parasite slots on news sites, and this guide is not a how-to for renting them. It is a map of the tactic, the policy line, and the work that still holds up.

Why host pages ranked

Google does not rank “brands.” It ranks URLs. A new domain with no links starts near zero. A 20-year news domain already has crawl budget, inbound links, and branded searches. Drop a money page on that host and the URL inherits a lot of that trust — at least until spam systems look at whether the host actually stands behind the page.

That is why affiliate, casino, crypto, and payday pages used to cluster on newspaper subfolders and .edu microsites. The query was commercial. The host was not. The snippet still won because the domain was famous. Rankings looked like digital PR. The business model was renting a URL.

Spam updates still hit this pattern. If your own site dropped in a confirmed rollout, audit policy first using the August 2026 spam update notes — do not assume a parasite URL will catch the traffic you lost.

Earned placement vs abuse

The useful test is not “is this on another domain?” Every LinkedIn post is. The test is who controls the page, why it exists, and whether the host’s audience would expect it.

Signal Earned / first-party profile Site reputation abuse
Who edits the page? You, or a journalist on staff A paid third party; host barely reviews it
Would the host’s readers expect this? Yes — news, interviews, your own profile No — casino, coupons, or loans on a city paper
Why does the URL exist? Inform, quote, or host your identity Rank a money query on borrowed authority
Can you keep it if the host changes policy? Profiles you own, or a published story The section disappears when the deal ends
GEO side effect Models can corroborate your entity Thin commercial copy that does not match the host

That last row matters for AI search. ChatGPT and Perplexity often check third-party sources before they name a business — maps, reviews, Wikipedia, Reddit. That is corroboration, not a parasite play. I mapped that list in 37 places AI checks before recommending you. Filling a real LinkedIn and a real Google Business Profile is GEO. Buying a coupon folder on a university domain is not.

What Google now punishes

Google added site reputation abuse to its spam policies in March 2024 and has kept enforcing it through later spam-system refreshes. In plain language: third-party pages published on an established site, mainly to rank in Google, with little first-party oversight. Google’s examples have included educational sites hosting commercial pages, and coupon or affiliate sections that sit apart from the host’s real editorial work.

Related policies that often travel with parasite campaigns:

  • Expired domain abuse — buying a dead host and turning it into a ranking shell.
  • Scaled content abuse — templated or AI-filled pages whose only job is to rank.
  • Link spam — paying for links, including guest posts whose real product is the backlink, not the article.

If a client asks “can we just put this on a DA-90 news site?”, the honest answer in 2026 is: that product still gets sold, and Google still has a named policy against the version where the host is a billboard. I would rather they cannot find your money page on a newspaper they have never read.

A bylined expert quote in a reported story is not site reputation abuse. A 2,000-word affiliate roundup on unrelated-city-times.com/best-crypto-casino/ with no newsroom involvement is the pattern the policy was written for.

Hosts people still use

Not every third-party URL is a rented parasite. These are the hosts I still treat as normal off-site SEO — because you either own the profile or a human edited the piece.

Profiles you control

  • LinkedIn — company page plus a real article under your name. Same entity as the website. Useful for GEO and for people, not a substitute for ranking your own domain.
  • YouTube — the video can rank in Google and in YouTube. You own the channel. Descriptions should match the site’s facts, not a keyword dump.
  • Google Business Profile — local pack, not a blog host. Still the highest-leverage “someone else’s domain” most local brands will ever use. See the GMB guide.

Mentions you earn

  • News and trade press — a reporter covers a launch, a study, or a quote. You do not control the URL. That is the point.
  • Guest posts with editorial standards — a real editor, a byline, a topic the host actually covers. One thoughtful piece beats twenty paid “contributor” URLs.
  • Community answers — Reddit, Quora, niche forums, used as a person, not a content farm. Scaled comment spam is still spam.

Platforms like Medium, Substack, and Behance sit in the middle. A unique essay under your name is a profile. Syndicating the same thin affiliate page across ten Medium accounts is scaled content on a host you do not own. Google does not need a new name for that.

A safer off-site playbook

If the goal is visibility — Google, Maps, and AI answers — build authority you can keep. Five steps I actually run with clients:

  1. Rank your own site first. Technical SEO, original pages, and a stable entity beat a URL you will lose when the invoice stops. Pair that with GEO if you care about ChatGPT citations, not only blue links.
  2. Earn the mention. Original data, expert quotes, and a story a journalist can verify. Digital PR is parasite-shaped on the SERP and policy-safe when the newsroom did the work.
  3. Own the profiles. LinkedIn, YouTube, GBP, Crunchbase if you are a company. Same NAP, same name, same city — Sta. Rosa, Laguna for me. Models and Google both use those as corroboration.
  4. Skip rented sections. If the host would not publish the page for their own readers, do not pay to park it there. “DA 90” is not a strategy. It is a risk label.
  5. Clean up old buys. Ask the host to remove the URLs. If they refuse and the links are junk pointing at you, that is a disavow question, not a ranking tactic to double down on.

If you already bought parasite URLs

Treat it like a spam-policy cleanup, not a secret channel to milk for one more quarter.

  • Inventory every third-party money URL you paid for. Note host, topic, and whether it still ranks.
  • Request removal. Screenshot the conversation. 301s to your own site are better than leaving a policy-bait page live.
  • Do not move the same thin affiliate copy onto the next news domain. That is how networks get mapped.
  • Rebuild the query on a page you own: unique data, clear authorship, and the same facts you want AI tools to cite.
  • If rankings on your domain also fell in a spam update, follow the recovery path in the August 2026 guide — content and policy, not more host shopping.

Parasite SEO as a product still gets pitched in Slack threads. The durable version is boring: earn coverage, keep profiles, and make your own site the URL you are willing to defend in Search Console.

Parasite SEO FAQ

Is parasite SEO still working in 2026?

Some host URLs still rank. Google’s site reputation abuse policy, plus later spam updates, made rented commercial sections on news and edu sites much less reliable. Earned press and profiles you control still work because they are not that product.

Is a LinkedIn article parasite SEO?

It is off-site. It is not site reputation abuse. You own the profile, LinkedIn expects articles there, and the page supports your entity. Use it as corroboration, not as a replacement for ranking your website.

Is guest posting spam?

Not if a real editor wanted the piece for their audience. It is spam when the article exists to place a keyworded link or a money page the host would not otherwise publish. Google’s link-spam and site-reputation rules both apply.

Does parasite SEO help AI citations?

Thin commercial pages on unrelated hosts rarely help ChatGPT or Perplexity name you. Consistent profiles, reviews, and reported articles do. That is the 37-places corroboration model, not a rented newspaper folder.

Should I buy a high-DA news placement?

I would not, if the host is renting a commercial section with little editorial control. Pay for reporting, sponsorships that are labeled, or ads. Do not rent a URL to evade building your own domain.
